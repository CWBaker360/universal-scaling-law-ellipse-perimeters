\begin{abstract}
For the ellipse \(q(\theta)=(a\cos\theta,b\sin\theta)\), \(a\ge b>0\), let \(P_N\) be the perimeter of the equal-parameter inscribed \(N\)-gon and let \(M_N\) be the composite midpoint approximation to the exact perimeter integral formed from the speed \(v(\theta)=(a^2\sin^2\theta+b^2\cos^2\theta)^{1/2}\). We prove the exact factorization \(P_N=\sinc(\pi/N)M_N\). For \(0<b<a\), the nearest complex zeros of the squared speed occur at imaginary distance \(\rho_*=\operatorname{artanh}(b/a)\), and Fourier aliasing gives \(M_N-P=O(e^{-\rho N})\) for every \(0<\rho<\rho_*\). It follows that, for every fixed \(r\ge1\), \((P-P_N)/P=\sum_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{k+1}\pi^{2k}/((2k+1)!N^{2k})+O(N^{-2r-2})+O(e^{-\rho N})\). Thus all algebraic coefficients in the relative polygonal perimeter error are independent of the ellipse axes; shape enters through the exponentially small midpoint remainder and the distance to the nearest complex singularity. Dividing the polygon perimeter by the sinc factor exactly removes the full algebraic chord error, leaving only exponentially convergent midpoint quadrature. Since the speed is also $\pi$-periodic, odd numbers of vertices have a doubled exponential aliasing rate. Classical scale-three Richardson cancellation is retained for comparison with geometric N-series constructions.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textit{Keywords:} ellipse perimeter; inscribed polygon; midpoint quadrature; periodic quadrature; Fourier aliasing; asymptotic error expansion.

\section{Introduction and claim boundary}

Polygonal approximation of a smooth closed curve naturally mixes two
effects: the geometry of replacing an arc by a chord, and the numerical
quadrature implicit in sampling the curve.  For an ellipse sampled at
equal parameter increments, these two effects separate exactly.

The perimeter of an ellipse has a long history of exact series,
inequalities, and closed-form approximations.  Ivory's classical work
gave a rapidly convergent series for rectification
\citet{Ivory1798}; later approximation formulas include those associated
with Peano and Ramanujan, with modern error analyses and historical
discussion available in, for example, \citet{Villarino2006}.  These
approaches concern approximation of the perimeter itself.  The present paper instead studies a specific equal-parameter inscribed
polygon as a numerical perimeter approximation and identifies an exact
factorization linking its geometric chord error to midpoint quadrature
of the analytic speed.

The central identity of this paper is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:central}
P_N=
\sinc\!\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)M_N,
\end{equation}
where \(P_N\) is the equal-parameter inscribed polygon perimeter and
\(M_N\) is the composite midpoint approximation to the perimeter
integral.  The factor \(\sinc(\pi/N)\) is universal, whereas all
shape dependence is contained in the midpoint sum.  Combining this
identity with exponential convergence of periodic analytic quadrature
shows that every algebraic coefficient in the relative polygonal
perimeter error is independent of the ellipse axes.

The analytic quadrature mechanism is classical.  Standard references
for numerical integration include Davis and Rabinowitz
\citet{DavisRabinowitz1984}; the exponential convergence of periodic
trapezoidal rules for analytic functions, together with its Fourier
and aliasing interpretation, is treated systematically by Trefethen
and Weideman \citet{TrefethenWeideman2014}.  Related contour estimates
for both trapezoidal and midpoint rules are given by
Javed and Trefethen \citet{JavedTrefethen2014}; earlier general contour-based quadrature remainder analysis appears in \citet{DonaldsonElliott1972}.

The contribution is therefore not a new quadrature or extrapolation
method.  Richardson-type cancellation of algebraic error terms is
classical.  The purpose here is to isolate the exact geometric
factorization \eqref{eq:central}, derive its consequences, and exhibit
the resulting universal algebraic coefficients.  Historical priority
for the bare factorization \eqref{eq:central} is not asserted here.

For a circle \(a=b=R\), the usual regular-polygon identity is
\[
P_N=2NR\sin\frac{\pi}{N}
=
2\pi R\,\sinc\!\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right).
\]
The ellipse identity below may be viewed as the variable-speed analogue:
the exact circumference \(2\pi R\) is replaced by a midpoint sampling
of the nonconstant ellipse speed.

\section{Ellipse geometry and equal-parameter polygons}

Fix
\[
a\ge b>0
\]
and parametrize the ellipse by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:q}
q(\theta)=(a\cos\theta,b\sin\theta),
\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi.
\end{equation}
Its speed is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:v}
v(\theta)
=
\|q'(\theta)\|
=
\sqrt{a^2\sin^2\theta+b^2\cos^2\theta}.
\end{equation}
The exact perimeter is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:P}
P=\int_0^{2\pi}v(\theta)\,\dd\theta.
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\[
P=4a\,E\!\left(1-\frac{b^2}{a^2}\right),
\]
where \(E\) denotes the complete elliptic integral of the second kind
in parameter notation.

For \(N\ge2\), set
\[
h=\frac{2\pi}{N},
\qquad
\theta_j=jh,
\qquad
j=0,\dots,N-1,
\]
with \(\theta_N=2\pi\).  The perimeter of the equal-parameter inscribed
polygon is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:PN}
P_N
=
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
\|q(\theta_{j+1})-q(\theta_j)\|.
\end{equation}
Define also the composite midpoint approximation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:MN}
M_N
=
h\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
v\!\left(\theta_j+\frac h2\right).
\end{equation}

\section{Exact chord factorization}

\begin{theorem}[Exact chord factorization]
\label{thm:factorization}
For every \(a\ge b>0\) and every integer \(N\ge2\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:factorization}
\boxed{
P_N=
\sinc\!\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)M_N.
}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For arbitrary real \(\theta\) and \(h\),
\[
\cos(\theta+h)-\cos\theta
=
-2\sin\!\left(\theta+\frac h2\right)\sin\frac h2
\]
and
\[
\sin(\theta+h)-\sin\theta
=
2\cos\!\left(\theta+\frac h2\right)\sin\frac h2.
\]
Hence
\begin{align*}
\|q(\theta+h)-q(\theta)\|^2
&=
4\sin^2\frac h2
\left[
a^2\sin^2\!\left(\theta+\frac h2\right)
+
b^2\cos^2\!\left(\theta+\frac h2\right)
\right] \\
&=
4\sin^2\frac h2\,
v\!\left(\theta+\frac h2\right)^2.
\end{align*}
For \(N\ge2\), \(h=2\pi/N\) and \(\sin(h/2)\ge0\), so
\[
\|q(\theta+h)-q(\theta)\|
=
2\sin\frac h2\,
v\!\left(\theta+\frac h2\right).
\]
Summing at \(\theta=\theta_j\) gives
\[
P_N
=
2\sin\frac{\pi}{N}
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
v\!\left(\theta_j+\frac{\pi}{N}\right).
\]
Since
\[
M_N
=
\frac{2\pi}{N}
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
v\!\left(\theta_j+\frac{\pi}{N}\right),
\]
we obtain
\[
P_N
=
\frac{\sin(\pi/N)}{\pi/N}\,M_N.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization} is exact.  No limiting argument,
Taylor expansion, or extrapolation method enters its proof.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Literature position]
The identity is derived here directly from the standard trigonometric
parametrization of the ellipse.  Classical ellipse-perimeter formulas
and series, such as those discussed in
\citep{Ivory1798,Villarino2006}, address the perimeter through elliptic
integrals, series, inequalities, or closed-form approximations.  No
claim of historical priority for the factorization
\eqref{eq:factorization} is made.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Exact sinc normalization and exponentially small residual]
\label{cor:sinc}
For $a\ge b>0$ and $N\ge2$ define the sinc-normalized perimeter
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sinc-corrected}
\boxed{\widetilde P_N:=\frac{P_N}{\sinc(\pi/N)}
=\frac{\pi P_N}{N\sin(\pi/N)}=M_N.}
\end{equation}
For each fixed noncircular ellipse $0<b<a$ and each
$0<\rho<\rho_*$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sinc-error}
\widetilde P_N-P=O(e^{-\rho N}).
\end{equation}
For a circle, $\widetilde P_N=P$ identically.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The equality in \eqref{eq:sinc-corrected} follows directly from
Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization}. Equation~\eqref{eq:sinc-error}
then follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:midpoint}. For $a=b$, the speed
is constant, so the midpoint rule is exact.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}[Computational versus geometric correction]
The normalization uses an evaluation of $\sin(\pi/N)$ and a division;
it is not asserted to be a straightedge-and-compass operation. It
removes the entire sinc-related algebraic error exactly, rather than
merely cancelling finitely many powers of $N^{-2}$. Its advantage is
asymptotic for each fixed $b/a>0$: if $N\rho_*$ is small, the remaining
quadrature error may still be significant.
\end{remark}

\section{Complex singularities of the ellipse speed}

Assume first that \(0<b<a\).  Consider
\[
F(z)=a^2\sin^2z+b^2\cos^2z.
\]
The complex zeros of \(F\) determine the obstruction to analytic
continuation of the branch of \(\sqrt{F}\) that equals \(v\) on the
real axis.

\begin{theorem}[Nearest complex singularity distance]
\label{thm:strip}
Let \(0<b<a\) and define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rhostar}
\boxed{
\rho_*=
\operatorname{artanh}\!\left(\frac ba\right)
=
\frac12\log\!\left(\frac{a+b}{a-b}\right).
}
\end{equation}
Then \(F(z)\neq0\) throughout
\[
|\operatorname{Im}z|<\rho_*,
\]
and zeros occur on the boundary at
\[
z=n\pi\pm i\rho_*,
\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Consequently, the real-positive speed \(v\) extends to an analytic
branch on every closed substrip
\[
|\operatorname{Im}z|\le\rho,
\qquad 0<\rho<\rho_*.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The equation \(F(z)=0\) is equivalent, whenever \(\cos z\neq0\), to
\[
a^2\tan^2z+b^2=0,
\]
hence
\[
\tan z=\pm i\frac ba.
\]
Using
\[
\tan(iy)=i\tanh y,
\]
we obtain the solutions
\[
z=n\pi\pm i\operatorname{artanh}\!\left(\frac ba\right),
\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
\]
No zero lies closer to the real axis.  Thus \(F\) is nonvanishing in
the open strip \(|\operatorname{Im}z|<\rho_*\).  Because this strip is
simply connected, the nonvanishing analytic function \(F\) admits an
analytic square root there, uniquely fixed by requiring agreement with
the positive real speed \(v\) on the real axis.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Circle case]
If \(a=b=R\), then \(v(\theta)\equiv R\) is entire and
\(M_N=P=2\pi R\) exactly.  The formula \(\rho_*=\operatorname{artanh}(b/a)\)
is then interpreted as the limiting value \(+\infty\).
\end{remark}

\section{Periodic midpoint quadrature}

For the ellipse speed, periodicity and continuity on each closed substrip
$|\operatorname{Im}z|\le\rho<\rho_*$ imply the finite bound $B_\rho$
used below.\par

We now give a self-contained Fourier proof of the exponential midpoint
remainder.  This avoids requiring a boundary estimate at the singular
lines \(|\operatorname{Im}z|=\rho_*\).

\begin{lemma}[Fourier coefficient decay]
\label{lem:fourierdecay}
Let \(f\) be \(2\pi\)-periodic and analytic in
\(|\operatorname{Im}z|<\rho_*\).  Fix \(0<\rho<\rho_*\), and suppose
\(f\) is bounded on \(|\operatorname{Im}z|\le\rho\) by \(B_\rho\).
If
\[
\widehat f_k
=
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(\theta)e^{-ik\theta}\,\dd\theta,
\]
then
\[
|\widehat f_k|
\le
B_\rho e^{-\rho|k|}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For \(k>0\), shift the contour from the real axis to
\(\operatorname{Im}z=-\rho\); periodicity cancels the vertical sides.
Then
\[
\widehat f_k
=
\frac1{2\pi}
\int_0^{2\pi}
f(\theta-i\rho)e^{-ik(\theta-i\rho)}\,\dd\theta,
\]
so
\[
|\widehat f_k|\le B_\rho e^{-k\rho}.
\]
For \(k<0\), shift instead to \(\operatorname{Im}z=+\rho\).
The case \(k=0\) is immediate.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exponential midpoint remainder]
\label{thm:midpoint}
For every fixed ellipse \(0<b<a\) and every
\(0<\rho<\rho_*\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:miderror}
\boxed{
M_N-P=O(e^{-\rho N}).
}
\end{equation}
The implied constant may depend on \(a,b,\rho\), but not on \(N\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write the Fourier series
\[
v(\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\widehat v_k e^{ik\theta}.
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:fourierdecay}, the series converges absolutely on the
real axis.  Since
\[
P=2\pi\widehat v_0,
\]
and the midpoint nodes are
\[
\theta_j^*=\frac{2\pi}{N}\left(j+\frac12\right),
\]
we have
\begin{align*}
M_N
&=
\frac{2\pi}{N}
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}
\widehat v_k e^{ik\theta_j^*} \\
&=
2\pi
\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z}
(-1)^m\widehat v_{mN},
\end{align*}
because the discrete sum vanishes unless \(k\) is a multiple of \(N\),
and
\[
e^{i(mN)\pi/N}=(-1)^m.
\]
Therefore
\[
M_N-P
=
2\pi
\sum_{m\ne0}
(-1)^m\widehat v_{mN}.
\]
For fixed \(0<\rho<\rho_*\), Lemma~\ref{lem:fourierdecay} gives
\[
|M_N-P|
\le
4\pi B_\rho
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e^{-\rho mN}
=
4\pi B_\rho
\frac{e^{-\rho N}}{1-e^{-\rho N}},
\]
which proves \eqref{eq:miderror}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Parity-refined aliasing of the midpoint rule]
\label{prop:parity}
Let $0<b<a$. For every $0<\rho<\rho_*$ the sinc-normalized
perimeter satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parity-bound}
\widetilde P_N-P=\begin{cases}
O(e^{-\rho N}),&N\text{ even},\\
O(e^{-2\rho N}),&N\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The implied constants may depend on $a,b,\rho$ but not on $N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The speed has period $\pi$, so $v(\theta+\pi)=v(\theta)$ and
$\widehat v_k=(-1)^k\widehat v_k$. Hence $\widehat v_k=0$
for all odd $k$. The midpoint aliasing identity established in the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:midpoint} is
\[
M_N-P=2\pi\sum_{m\ne0}(-1)^m\widehat v_{mN}.
\]
If $N$ is even, the first potentially nonzero terms have $m=\pm1$;
if $N$ is odd, $m$ must be even, so the first potentially nonzero
terms have $m=\pm2$. For every $0<\rho<\rho_*$ the coefficient
bound $|\widehat v_k|\le B_\rho e^{-\rho|k|}$ implies, respectively,
\[
|M_N-P|\le\frac{4\pi B_\rho e^{-\rho N}}
{1-e^{-\rho N}}\quad(N\text{ even}),
\]
\[
|M_N-P|\le\frac{4\pi B_\rho e^{-2\rho N}}
{1-e^{-2\rho N}}\quad(N\text{ odd}).
\]
Use $M_N=\widetilde P_N$ to finish.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}[Interpretation of the doubled rate]
The exponent is doubled at odd $N$ because the $\pi$-periodic speed
has only even Fourier harmonics. The estimates hold for every strict
substrip $\rho<\rho_*$; they do not assert a pure exponential
leading asymptotic exactly at the branch-point boundary. Odd $N$
can therefore be especially effective once $N\rho_*$ is large,
although no universal advantage at small $N$ is claimed.
\end{remark}

\section{Universal relative-error expansion}

The exact geometry and exponential quadrature estimate now combine.

\begin{theorem}[Universal finite-order relative-error expansion]
\label{thm:universal}
Fix \(a\ge b>0\).  If \(0<b<a\), choose any
\(0<\rho<\rho_*\); if \(a=b\), omit the exponential term below.
Then for every fixed integer \(r\ge1\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:universal}
\boxed{
\frac{P-P_N}{P}
=
\sum_{k=1}^{r}
(-1)^{k+1}
\frac{\pi^{2k}}{(2k+1)!N^{2k}}
+
O(N^{-2r-2})
+
O(e^{-\rho N}).
}
\end{equation}
In particular, every algebraic coefficient is independent of \(a\)
and \(b\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization},
\[
\frac{P_N}{P}
=
\sinc\!\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)
\frac{M_N}{P}.
\]
For \(0<b<a\), Theorem~\ref{thm:midpoint} yields
\[
\frac{M_N}{P}
=
1+O(e^{-\rho N}).
\]
Since \(\sinc(\pi/N)\) is bounded,
\[
\frac{P_N}{P}
=
\sinc\!\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)
+
O(e^{-\rho N}).
\]
Now
\[
\sinc x
=
\sum_{k=0}^{r}
(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k+1)!}
+
O(x^{2r+2})
\qquad (x\to0).
\]
Substituting \(x=\pi/N\), subtracting from \(1\), and collecting terms
gives \eqref{eq:universal}.  In the circle case, \(M_N=P\) exactly, so
the same algebraic expansion follows without an exponential remainder.
\end{proof}

The first few terms are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:firstterms}
\frac{P-P_N}{P}
=
\frac{\pi^2}{6N^2}
-\frac{\pi^4}{120N^4}
+\frac{\pi^6}{5040N^6}
-\frac{\pi^8}{362880N^8}
+O(N^{-10})
+O(e^{-\rho N}).
\end{equation}

\begin{corollary}[Universal leading constant]
\label{cor:leading}
For every fixed ellipse \(a\ge b>0\),
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\lim_{N\to\infty}
N^2\frac{P-P_N}{P}
=
\frac{\pi^2}{6}.
}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Universal second coefficient]
For every fixed ellipse \(a\ge b>0\),
\[
\lim_{N\to\infty}
N^4
\left[
\frac{P-P_N}{P}
-\frac{\pi^2}{6N^2}
\right]
=
-\frac{\pi^4}{120}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\section{Shape dependence and asymptotic onset}

Theorems~\ref{thm:factorization} and~\ref{thm:universal} separate the
two roles played by geometry.
The algebraic coefficients arise entirely from the universal chord
factor
\[
\sinc\!\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right),
\]
whereas the ellipse shape enters through the midpoint remainder.

For \(0<b<a\),
\[
\rho_*=
\operatorname{artanh}\!\left(\frac ba\right)
\]
is the distance to the nearest complex zero of
\(a^2\sin^2z+b^2\cos^2z\).  As \(b/a\downarrow0\),
\[
\rho_*\downarrow0.
\]
Thus highly eccentric ellipses possess a narrower strip of analyticity,
and the exponentially small midpoint contribution becomes visible over
a longer pre-asymptotic range.

This yields the structural interpretation
\[
\boxed{
\text{universal algebraic coefficients}
\quad+\quad
\text{shape-dependent exponential onset}.
}
\]
No explicit finite-\(N\) onset threshold is claimed here.

\section{Classical Richardson cancellation as a corollary}

We now illustrate how the universal expansion may be used by a standard
extrapolation step.  This section is deliberately secondary: the
cancellation method is classical, while Theorem~\ref{thm:universal}
supplies the geometric expansion to which it is applied.

\begin{corollary}[Scale-\(3\) Richardson cancellation]
\label{cor:richardson}
Define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rich}
\widehat P_N
=
\frac{9P_{3N}-P_N}{8}.
\end{equation}
Then, for every fixed \(0<b<a\) and every \(0<\rho<\rho_*\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:richerror}
\boxed{
\frac{P-\widehat P_N}{P}
=
\frac{\pi^4}{1080N^4}
-
\frac{\pi^6}{40824N^6}
+
O(N^{-8})
+
O(e^{-\rho N}).
}
\end{equation}
For a circle the exponential term is absent.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write
\[
E_N=\frac{P-P_N}{P}
=
\frac{A}{N^2}
+\frac{B}{N^4}
+\frac{C}{N^6}
+O(N^{-8})
+O(e^{-\rho N}),
\]
where
\[
A=\frac{\pi^2}{6},
\qquad
B=-\frac{\pi^4}{120},
\qquad
C=\frac{\pi^6}{5040}.
\]
From \eqref{eq:rich},
\[
\frac{P-\widehat P_N}{P}
=
\frac{9E_{3N}-E_N}{8}.
\]
The \(N^{-2}\) term cancels exactly.  The remaining coefficients are
\[
-\frac{B}{9}
=
\frac{\pi^4}{1080}
\]
and
\[
-\frac{10C}{81}
=
-\frac{\pi^6}{40824}.
\]
The exponential contribution remains exponentially small.
\end{proof}

They do not imply superiority to optimized direct algorithms for
complete elliptic integrals, and they do not replace the geometric
motivation for studying the scale-three extrapolation.

\section{Extensions and open questions}

The exact factorization in Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization} relies on the
specific trigonometric parametrization of the ellipse.  Several natural
questions remain.

First, one may ask which other parametrized closed curves admit an
exact decomposition of chord lengths into a universal mesh factor and
a sampled local speed or related geometric density.  Second, the
boundary singularities at \(|\operatorname{Im}z|=\rho_*\) have a
square-root character; a sharper singularity analysis may yield a
more precise asymptotic form than the deliberately conservative
\(O(e^{-\rho N})\) estimate for \(\rho<\rho_*\).  Third, the behavior of
the approximation in simultaneous limits \(N\to\infty\) and \(b/a\to0\)
is not covered by the fixed-shape theorem proved here.

No claim is made here concerning arbitrary spherical domains, modular
transformations, or a general equivalence between geometric scale
cancellation and other acceleration mechanisms.

\subsection{Numerical evidence for a sharper midpoint asymptotic}
\label{sec:sharp-conjecture}

For fixed $0<b<a$, write $\rho_*=\operatorname{artanh}(b/a)$ and
$C(a,b)=4\sqrt{2\pi ab}$.  The nearest zeros of the squared speed are
simple and give square-root branch points for $v$.  A Darboux-type
analysis therefore suggests the \emph{conjectural} leading laws
\begin{align}
 M_N-P &\sim C(a,b)N^{-3/2}e^{-\rho_*N}, &&N\ \text{even},\label{eq:sharp-even}\\
 M_N-P &\sim -C(a,b)(2N)^{-3/2}e^{-2\rho_*N},&&N\ \text{odd}.\label{eq:sharp-odd}
\end{align}
The parity change is consistent with the exact aliasing identity and
vanishing of the odd Fourier modes: for odd $N$, the first nonzero alias
occurs at frequency $2N$ with a positive midpoint phase, whereas for
even $N$ it occurs at frequency $N$ with a negative midpoint phase.
These expressions are \emph{not} asserted as proved asymptotics here.

\section{Reproducibility appendix}

The numerical entries in Table~\ref{tab:numerics} can be reproduced
without numerical differencing of adjacent ellipse points.  For
\(a=1\) and a chosen \(b\), evaluate
\[
P=4E(1-b^2),
\]
then compute
\[
M_N
=
\frac{2\pi}{N}
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
\sqrt{
\sin^2\!\left(\left(j+\frac12\right)\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)
+
b^2
\cos^2\!\left(\left(j+\frac12\right)\frac{2\pi}{N}\right)
},
\]
followed by
\[
P_N=\sinc\!\left(\frac{\pi}{N}\right)M_N,
\qquad
\widehat P_N=\frac{9P_{3N}-P_N}{8}.
\]
High precision should be used when the corrected error is small.

The companion verification procedures can perform these computations
using arbitrary-precision arithmetic. The sharp-asymptotic diagnostic
uses the explicit midpoint sum with 120-digit working precision.
